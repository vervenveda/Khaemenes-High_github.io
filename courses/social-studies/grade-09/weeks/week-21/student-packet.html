Land-Based Empires, 1450–1750

Essential Question: How did large empires govern religious, linguistic, and ethnic diversity?

Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

Early modern land empires expanded through military organization, fiscal systems, dynastic alliances, and control of strategic routes. Gunpowder mattered, but states also needed administrators, food supplies, skilled workers, and agreements with local powerholders.

People, Power, and Systems

The Ottoman Empire governed Muslims, Christians, Jews, and many language groups through layered institutions. Safavid rulers promoted Twelver Shiism as a state identity. Mughal emperors used revenue systems, military service, alliances, and changing religious policies. Ming and Qing rulers administered a vast agrarian economy, while Russia expanded across Eurasia.

Reading the Evidence

Palaces, mosques, forts, paintings, ceremonies, and titles projected universal authority. Yet petitions, tax registers, and provincial records reveal bargaining and resistance. Imperial rule was often strongest near administrative centers and weaker at frontiers.

Why It Matters

Studying several empires together prevents one-region narratives and highlights a recurring political problem: how a central government can govern diverse communities without unlimited resources.

Source Study: Imperial Image and Administrative Record

Compare a court image or monument with a tax, petition, or provincial record. Ask whether the image and record present the same kind of power.

Vocabulary

gunpowder empireA label for states whose expansion and defense used firearms and artillery, though technology alone did not explain success.
devshirmeAn Ottoman levy that recruited some Christian boys for state and military service.
milletA term often used for Ottoman arrangements granting recognized religious communities limited autonomy.
zamindarA revenue intermediary or landholder in Mughal South Asia.
absolutismA political doctrine or practice emphasizing concentrated monarchical authority.
legitimacyPublic acceptance or justification of a ruler’s right to govern.

Key Understandings

  • Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming-Qing, Russian, African, and European states used different combinations of military power, taxation, religion, and local elites.
  • Imperial diversity was managed through negotiation as well as coercion.
  • Court art and architecture communicated legitimacy but should be compared with tax, legal, and local records.
