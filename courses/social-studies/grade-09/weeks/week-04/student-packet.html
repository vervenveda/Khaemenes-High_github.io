Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

Some of the earliest large urban societies developed near the Tigris-Euphrates and Nile river systems. Rivers supplied water and fertile soil, but flooding patterns differed. Mesopotamian communities built canals and levees across competing city-states; Egyptian rulers coordinated resources along a long river corridor.

People, Power, and Systems

Cities concentrated population, craft production, worship, markets, and political authority. Administrators counted grain, recorded labor, collected taxes or tribute, and organized military and construction projects. Monumental buildings displayed religious and political power while also requiring thousands of coordinated workers.

Reading the Evidence

Law codes such as the collection associated with Hammurabi did not function exactly like a modern statute book. They present cases and penalties that reveal ideas about property, family, commerce, status, and justice. Egyptian administrative records, tomb scenes, and inscriptions provide different kinds of evidence about work and authority.

Why It Matters

Early states solved coordination problems but also formalized inequality. Studying them helps students ask enduring civic questions: Who writes laws? Whose interests are protected? How should public works be funded? What makes authority legitimate?

Source Study: Law and Administration in Mesopotamia and Egypt

Compare selected paraphrased cases from Hammurabi’s code with an Egyptian labor or tax record. Identify priorities, social categories, and limits.

Vocabulary

civilizationA complex society with cities, institutions, specialized labor, and systems of communication.
city-stateA city and its surrounding territory functioning as an independent political unit.
bureaucracyAn administrative system of officials and offices.
cuneiformA wedge-shaped writing system developed in ancient Mesopotamia.
tributeGoods, labor, or wealth delivered to a ruler or state.
rule of lawThe principle that authority is exercised through known laws rather than arbitrary command.

Key Understandings

  • River valleys supported intensive agriculture but required coordinated labor and management.
  • Writing and accounting expanded partly because governments and temples needed to track resources and obligations.
  • Early law codes reveal social priorities and inequalities as well as attempts to regulate conflict.
