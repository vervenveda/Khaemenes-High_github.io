Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

This week pauses chronological movement to compare societies across Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas, and Oceania. Students examine state formation, economic organization, social hierarchy, belief, technology, and environmental adaptation.

People, Power, and Systems

Comparison is not a contest to determine which society was most advanced. A useful comparison asks the same question of each case while explaining why differences emerged. It also avoids treating modern nation-states as timeless units.

Reading the Evidence

Students work with a source packet containing laws, traveler accounts, maps, objects, architecture, and quantitative estimates. They practice grouping documents, recognizing point of view, and building a thesis that answers a question rather than listing facts.

Why It Matters

Midcourse synthesis strengthens long-term memory and prepares students for the midterm. More importantly, it shows that evidence can support multiple defensible arguments when reasoning is explicit.

Source Study: Mini Document-Based Question

Use eight sources from at least five regions to answer a comparison question about political legitimacy or trade.

Vocabulary

comparisonExamining meaningful similarities and differences using consistent categories.
causationExplaining how and why one condition contributes to an outcome.
continuityA feature that persists across a defined period.
periodizationDividing history into periods to organize change.
state capacityThe ability of a government to carry out decisions, collect resources, and enforce rules.
historical argumentA claim supported by selected evidence and explicit reasoning.

Key Understandings

  • Global comparison requires the same analytical categories while preserving regional context.
  • States built capacity through taxation, labor systems, military force, belief, bureaucracy, and negotiated local authority.
  • Exchange networks connected regions unevenly and produced both opportunity and vulnerability.

Daily Lessons

Monday · Frame the Inquiry

Objective: Locate Global Comparison, 1200–1450 in time and space and develop an initial answer to the essential question.

Warm-up: Without notes, write three observations or questions suggested by the title “Global Comparison, 1200–1450.”

Lesson: This week pauses chronological movement to compare societies across Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas, and Oceania. Students examine state formation, economic organization, social hierarchy, belief, technology, and environmental adaptation.

Guided practice: Build a class chronology for c. 1200–1450 CE and identify the focal region(s): Global. Mark what is known, inferred, or still questioned.

Independent practice: Complete the first section of the weekly inquiry notebook: initial claim, two questions, and a labeled time-space frame.

Exit check: Explain one way chronology or geography prevents a misleading interpretation.

Tuesday · Read Like a Historian

Objective: Source, contextualize, and corroborate primary and secondary evidence.

Warm-up: Preview the source study: Mini Document-Based Question. Predict its possible audience and purpose.

Lesson: Comparison is not a contest to determine which society was most advanced. A useful comparison asks the same question of each case while explaining why differences emerged. It also avoids treating modern nation-states as timeless units.

Guided practice: Use eight sources from at least five regions to answer a comparison question about political legitimacy or trade.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 1, identifying origin, audience, purpose, context, claims, useful evidence, limits, and one corroborating source needed.

Exit check: State one claim the source can support and one claim it cannot support by itself.

Wednesday · Map and Measure

Objective: Use geographic and quantitative evidence to explain patterns and relationships.

Warm-up: Study the map or data before reading its title. List two patterns and one question.

Lesson: Students work with a source packet containing laws, traveler accounts, maps, objects, architecture, and quantitative estimates. They practice grouping documents, recognizing point of view, and building a thesis that answers a question rather than listing facts.

Guided practice: Create a thematic map using one symbol system consistently across all world regions.

Independent practice: Build a comparison table for taxation, labor, military organization, trade, social hierarchy, and belief.

Exit check: Describe one pattern, one plausible explanation, and one limitation of the map or data.

Thursday · Discuss, Debate, and Write

Objective: Construct an evidence-based argument that acknowledges a competing interpretation.

Warm-up: Choose an initial position on: Which comparison category—government, economy, belief, geography, or technology—best explains regional differences?

Lesson: Midcourse synthesis strengthens long-term memory and prepares students for the midterm. More importantly, it shows that evidence can support multiple defensible arguments when reasoning is explicit.

Guided practice: Use a claim-evidence-reasoning organizer. Require evidence from the weekly reading, source study, and map or data lab.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 3 as a structured seminar contribution, policy memo, or evidence-based response.

Exit check: Revise the claim after hearing a counterargument and explain what changed.

Friday · Synthesize and Demonstrate

Objective: Synthesize the week’s historical content and disciplinary skills.

Warm-up: Retrieve six key terms and three major ideas without notes; then correct in another color.

Lesson: Review the essential question through chronology, geography, institutions, economy, culture, and evidence. Connect the week to an earlier course theme.

Guided practice: Complete a four-corner synthesis: continuity, change, cause, and consequence.

Independent practice: Complete the weekly quiz and short response, then record corrections and a next-step goal.

Exit check: Answer the essential question in two sentences: Which patterns of power, exchange, belief, and inequality best explain the world of 1200–1450?

Three Assignments

Assignment 1 · Source Lab — Mini Document-Based Question

Use eight sources from at least five regions to answer a comparison question about political legitimacy or trade. Complete an OPVL analysis: origin, purpose, value, and limitation. Then write a 150–250 word response explaining what the source contributes to the essential question.

Deliverables: Completed source-analysis chart; One evidence-based paragraph; One corroboration question

Assignment 2 · Atlas and Data Investigation

Create a thematic map using one symbol system consistently across all world regions. Then Build a comparison table for taxation, labor, military organization, trade, social hierarchy, and belief. Submit a labeled product and a 120–200 word interpretation that states a pattern, explanation, and limitation.

Deliverables: Labeled map, graph, table, or model; Interpretive paragraph; Source note

Assignment 3 · Evidence-Based Seminar or Argument

Respond to: Which comparison category—government, economy, belief, geography, or technology—best explains regional differences? Use at least three pieces of evidence, include one counterclaim or qualification, and conclude with the broader historical significance.

Deliverables: Claim or thesis; Three cited pieces of evidence; Counterclaim and response; Conclusion
