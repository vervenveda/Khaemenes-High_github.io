Atlantic Slavery, Colonialism, and Resistance

Essential Question: How did racial slavery become embedded in Atlantic economies, and how did enslaved people resist?

Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

The Atlantic slave trade forcibly transported millions of Africans over more than three centuries. European traders, African political actors, merchants, and American plantation owners participated in a changing system, but European colonial demand and shipping networks drove its enormous expansion.

People, Power, and Systems

Plantation economies produced sugar, tobacco, rice, coffee, and cotton for distant markets. Colonial laws defined people as property and made status inheritable through the mother in many jurisdictions. Racial ideologies developed to justify exploitation and unequal rights.

Reading the Evidence

Ship records quantify voyages but reduce people to categories. Narratives, court petitions, advertisements, songs, archaeology, and family histories restore individual experience while showing source limits. Resistance occurred every day as well as in famous rebellions.

Why It Matters

Atlantic slavery shaped wealth, law, race, migration, foodways, music, religion, and political debate across several continents. Its legacies did not end with legal abolition.

Source Study: Voyage Data and Human Testimony

Compare a ship manifest or database entry with a first-person narrative and a resistance account. Discuss what numbers reveal and conceal.

Vocabulary

chattel slaveryA system treating enslaved people as legally owned property, often for life and by descent.
Middle PassageThe forced Atlantic voyage carrying enslaved Africans to the Americas.
plantationA large agricultural enterprise relying on controlled labor to produce cash crops.
racializationThe process of assigning social meaning and hierarchy to perceived physical differences.
abolitionThe movement and process of ending slavery.
maroon communityA community established by people who escaped slavery.

Key Understandings

  • African societies had varied relationships to slavery, but Atlantic demand expanded warfare, capture, and human trafficking.
  • European colonial law increasingly made slavery racial, hereditary, and permanent.
  • Enslaved people resisted through survival, family, culture, work slowdowns, escape, revolt, legal petitions, and abolitionist organizing.
